# Recommendation

## Summary
Attrition is not evenly distributed — it is concentrated in a small, identifiable set of employee segments. The single largest driver is overtime: employees working overtime leave at **31%**, three times the rate of those who don't (**10%**). This effect compounds with role, income, age, and tenure, pointing to burnout and undercompensation among junior, high-workload staff as the core problem rather than attrition being a broad, company-wide issue.

## Where to focus
- **Sales, and Sales Representatives specifically** — Sales is the highest-attrition department (21%), and Sales Representative is the single highest-attrition role in the company (40%), well above every other role. This one role alone warrants a dedicated retention review.
- **Overtime employees** — the clearest, strongest signal in the data (31% vs 10%). Workload and overtime policy should be reviewed first, before any other intervention.
- **Low-income employees** — 25% attrition vs 9% for high earners. Combined with the overtime effect, this suggests employees are leaving because effort isn't being matched by pay.
- **Under-30 employees** — 28% attrition, roughly double every older age band. Early-career staff are the least anchored and most at risk.
- **Low work-life balance ratings** — employees rating their work-life balance a 1 leave at 31%, nearly double the rate at a 4.

## Recommended actions
1. Audit overtime hours and workload distribution in Sales, especially for the Sales Representative role — this is where the compounding risk is highest.
2. Review compensation bands for low-income employees, particularly where overtime is also high.
3. Build a structured early-career retention track (mentorship, faster promotion visibility) for under-30 hires.
4. Track work-life balance scores as a leading indicator, not just a satisfaction metric — it correlates directly with attrition here.

## Note on approach
This analysis was built as an agile, single-session Excel project: clean the data, build a lean model, layer in measures, and ship a working two-page dashboard fast rather than over-scoping. The recommendation above reflects what the current data supports; a deeper study (e.g. tenure-adjusted analysis, statistical significance testing) would be a natural next iteration, not a blocker to acting on these findings now.
